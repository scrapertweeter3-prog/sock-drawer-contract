The sock drawer is a thirty-year contract

A $3 pair of socks reads like the frugal choice until the drawer is priced in decades. Say a cheap pair wears through in about six months: two pairs a year, $6 a year, $60 a decade, $180 over thirty years to keep one slot in the drawer stocked. An $18 pair carrying a lifetime guarantee covers the same slot once, and the guarantee mails out a free replacement every time a pair fails, so wear stops being a recurring bill and becomes a one-time price. The drawer never sees the difference; the ledger does.

Break-even lands in year three, the point where the cheap rotation has burned its sixth $3 pair and matched the $18 paid once up front. From there the cheap slot keeps charging $6 a year and the guaranteed slot costs about $0.60 a year spread across three decades, a $5.40 annual gap that reaches $162 a slot by year thirty. A five-slot drawer carries the contract five times over: $810 for identical coverage, a number no price tag shows.

Cost per day closes it at small scale. The $3 pair worn 180 days runs about 1.7 cents a day. The $18 pair spread across its first decade runs about half a cent a day, and every free replacement under the guarantee pushes the rate down instead of restarting it. Ten years in, the cheap slot has paid $60 and owns nothing; the guaranteed slot paid $18 and still holds its contract.
